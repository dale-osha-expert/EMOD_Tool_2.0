# How to Read an Experience Mod Worksheet (and Spot Errors)

Your experience mod comes with two pages of numbers: the experience rating worksheet. Every figure that sets your workers' compensation mod is on it, from payroll by class code to the reserve on each workplace injury claim, and most of it can be checked with a calculator.

This guide shows how to read an experience modification worksheet from the National Council on Compensation Insurance (NCCI) field by field, using NCCI's own published sample. It shows the arithmetic you can check yourself, the errors that turn up most often, and what to do if you find one.

> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - The worksheet has a detail page (payroll, expected losses, and claims by policy) and a summary page that runs the formula. NCCI numbers 34 fields across the two.
> - You can check most of it by hand: expected losses are the expected loss rate times payroll divided by 100, and primary losses stop at the split point.
> - Medical-only claims show at full value on the detail page but are cut by 70% in the summary's lettered totals row, which is why your summary can show two different loss totals.
> - Where an error sits matters. On NCCI's sample, a $20,000 error in primary losses moves the mod about seven times more than the same error in excess losses.

## What Is an Experience Mod Worksheet?

An experience mod worksheet, also called an experience rating worksheet or experience modification worksheet, is the rating bureau's printout of the payroll, claims, and rating values used to calculate your experience modification rating. In most states, NCCI produces it.

The data comes from unit statistical reports that your insurer files with NCCI for each policy. NCCI's [ABCs of Experience Rating](https://www.ncci.com/Articles/Documents/UW_ABC_Exp_Rating.pdf) explains that insurers don't have to report a policy until 18 months after it starts, and that the mod is usually calculated 60 to 90 days before its effective date. That's why your current policy never appears on the worksheet. For how the numbers combine into a mod, see our guide to [how the experience mod formula works](https://emodanalyzer.com/what-is-experience-modification-rate-emr/).

## How Do You Get Your Experience Mod Worksheet?

Ask your insurance agent or carrier first. They receive your experience rating information, and they can explain anything that looks off. NCCI sends notification of each completed mod to the insurer on file "and, in some states, to the employer," and the mod and worksheet are available through its Riskworkstation application, which requires an account.

When Chesapeake Employers' Insurance in Maryland became fully affiliated with NCCI on January 1, 2023, it told policyholders that NCCI would send a letter explaining how to create an NCCI account about 120 days before renewal ([Chesapeake Employers](https://www.ceiwc.com/PDFs/Letter-to-Policyholders-NCCI-Experience-Rating-Worksheet.pdf)). States with their own bureaus have their own process:

- **California:** the WCIRB has a [policyholder worksheet request](https://www.wcirb.com/products-and-services/products-and-services/experience-modification-services/policyholder-experience-rating-worksheet-request) page for employers.
- **New Jersey:** the NJCRIB publishes [worksheet order forms](https://www.njcrib.com/DocumentLibrary/USR_XROrderForms).
- **New York:** the NYCIRB has its own [worksheet explainer](https://www.nycirb.org/exr-worksheet.php).

The rest of this guide uses NCCI's layout. Other bureaus' worksheets differ in format, but most of the same checks apply.

## The Top of the Worksheet: Risk Information

The header (fields 1 to 5) identifies the employer, state, risk identification number, production date ("when the mod was calculated"), and rating effective date ("the date the mod can be applied to the policy").

Each policy block on the detail page also shows the carrier, policy number, and policy period (effective and expiration dates), so check those against your own records too. Then check two things. First, the rating effective date should match your policy's renewal. Second, the policies listed should all have started between 21 and 57 months before that date; NCCI uses that window to define the experience period, usually three policy years.

Also look for the mod's status:

- **Preliminary:** NCCI didn't yet have your state's final approved rating values, so it used the prior ones. The mod will be revised.
- **Contingent:** NCCI was still waiting on audited payroll or loss data from your insurer. The mod will be revised once it arrives.
- **Final:** all approved values and data are in.

## Payroll, Class Codes, and Expected Losses

Each line on the left side of the detail page (fields 6 to 11) shows a class code, its payroll, its expected loss rate (ELR), the expected losses, the discount ratio (D-ratio), and the expected primary losses. Two calculations drive this block:

- **Expected losses** = ELR × payroll ÷ 100
- **Expected primary losses** = expected losses × D-ratio

You can't check the expected loss rates and D-ratios themselves; NCCI says they're actuarially derived and updated with each state-approved rate change. The checks that matter most aren't arithmetic, though. Are your classification codes right for the work your people actually do? A wrong code compares you against the wrong group of employers. And does the payroll match your audited payroll for that policy?

## Claims: Open vs. Final, Medical-Only, and the Split Point

The right side of the detail page (fields 12 to 19) lists claims data for each policy. Claims over $2,000 appear by claim number. Claims of $2,000 or less may be grouped by injury type and shown as "NO." followed by the count. Each line shows:

- **Injury type (IJ).** Type 6 is medical-only. NCCI reduces these by 70% under its Experience Rating Adjustment, which most states have approved, but only in the summary. The detail page shows the full amount.
- **Open or final (O/F).** An open claim counts at its current reserve, which is the insurer's estimate of what it will cost. If a claim has closed for less than its reserve, or is likely to, that's worth raising.
- **Actual incurred and actual primary losses.** Primary loss stops at the split point. On NCCI's sample, claim 1700001 shows $9,000 indemnity plus $40,985 medical, $49,985 in total, with $18,000 as primary under the split point NCCI used in that edition. NCCI has since moved to state-specific split points (Item E-1409, proposed for policies effective on or after July 1, 2024, with states adopting it on their own dates, per the [Texas Department of Insurance filing notice](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/rules/2022/documents/e1409notice.pdf)), so check yours.

## The Summary Page: Checking the Formula

The summary page (fields 20 to 34) totals everything and runs NCCI's formula. On NCCI's sample, every line checks out:

A result of 1.00, an EMR of 1.0, means this employer's actual losses matched its expected losses once the formula's weighting and stabilizing values were applied.

**Wt** is the weight given to excess losses. NCCI says it "is a small percentage for small employers and increases with the size of the employer." **Ballast** is added to both actual and expected losses to damp the effect of any one claim; NCCI says it "increases as expected losses increase." The last row can also show assigned-risk factors (ARAP and similar programs), which NCCI says mostly apply to assigned-risk policies with a debit mod.

<!-- [UNIQUE INSIGHT] -->
**Why your summary shows two loss totals.** On NCCI's sample summary, the state row shows actual incurred losses of $130,920 and actual primary losses of $98,935. The lettered totals row below it, (A) through (I), which feeds the formula, uses $100,569 and $68,584. Both totals drop by exactly $30,351, and excess losses ($31,985) are the same in both rows. That's consistent with the medical-only reduction, which NCCI says is "shown in the summary only."

On this sample, the medical-only claims were all under the split point, so cutting them by 70% reduced primary losses and left excess alone. If a medical-only claim is larger than the split point, its excess portion is cut by 70% too, so excess will drop as well. In a state that hasn't adopted the reduction, the two totals should match. If the gap doesn't line up with your medical-only claims, ask your agent why.

## Where an Error Sits Changes What It Costs

Not every dollar on the worksheet carries the same weight. Primary losses count in full. Excess losses count only at the Wt percentage.

<!-- [UNIQUE INSIGHT] -->
Using NCCI's sample values (adjusted expected losses of $223,590 and a weight of 0.14), take two $20,000 errors. Two $10,000 claims that were each entered twice add $20,000 of primary losses and raise the mod by about 0.089. If a $150,000 claim (below the state's per-claim limit) is reserved $20,000 too high, that adds $20,000 of excess losses and raises the mod by about 0.0125. On $100,000 of premium before the mod, that's roughly $8,900 a year versus $1,250.

## Common Experience Mod Worksheet Errors

The error types that mod specialists report are almost all data problems, not formula problems. A 2023 [Leader's Edge](https://www.leadersedge.com/p-c/experience-mod-sleuths) article on mod audits, from the magazine of The Council of Insurance Agents & Brokers, listed the kinds that experience mod specialists find:

- **Payroll errors**, which the article called among the largest categories
- **Wrong classification codes**
- **Preliminary data that was never updated** with final values
- **Duplicate claims or incorrect claim dates**
- **Non-compensable claims** left on the worksheet
- **Subrogation recoveries** not reflected
- **Deductibles** not handled correctly
- **Reserves that were never adjusted down**

Ownership changes can also cause problems. NCCI requires employers to notify their insurer in writing within 90 days of an ownership change, and it may issue, retract, or revise the current mod and up to two prior mods as a result.

How common are errors? Kelly Lopez, an experience modifier specialist at Insurance Office of America, told Leader's Edge that on average 5% to 10% of the mods she audits have errors of some sort. That's one specialist's experience, not a market-wide rate, but it's a reason to check.

## What to Do If You Find an Error

Start with your insurer, since a fix usually means correcting the data the insurer reported. The [Connecticut Insurance Department](https://portal.ct.gov/cid/resource-library/wc-dispute-resolution) lays out the path in that NCCI state:

1. **Work it out with your insurer.** The employer and insurance company should make a reasonable attempt to resolve the issue and keep records of their correspondence.
2. **Contact NCCI's Customer Service Center** (800-622-4123) if the dispute involves NCCI's manual rules. The department lists "application of an experience rating modification factor" as an example.
3. **NCCI's Dispute Resolution Process.** If it's still unresolved, the dispute may be eligible for this process.
4. **State appeal.** In Connecticut, a hearing goes before the state's appeals board, and the board's decision can be appealed to the Insurance Department within 30 days of the board's decision letter.

The later steps, and how far back a correction can reach, vary by state. Leader's Edge reported that four states have "aggravated inequity" rules that allow a broader review of mod determinations: Florida, Massachusetts, Minnesota, and Wisconsin. Ask your agent which rules apply to you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's on an NCCI experience rating worksheet?

The payroll, claims, and rating values behind your workers' comp experience mod. It has a detail page listing payroll, expected losses, and claims by policy, and a summary page that totals them and calculates the mod.

### How do I get my NCCI experience mod worksheet?

Ask your insurance agent or carrier. NCCI notifies the insurer on file, and in some states the employer, when a mod is completed, and the worksheet is available through NCCI's Riskworkstation with an account.

### What does "Wt" mean on a mod worksheet?

Wt is the weight given to excess losses, the part of each claim above the split point. It's small for small employers and larger for big ones, so larger employers' mods follow their own claims more closely.

### Why do the loss totals on my summary page differ?

Most often, the medical-only reduction. The detail page and the summary's state row show medical-only claims (injury type 6) at full value, but the lettered totals row that feeds the formula counts only 30% of them where your state applies NCCI's Experience Rating Adjustment. On NCCI's sample, the gap was $30,351, all in primary losses because the medical-only claims were under the split point.

## The Bottom Line

An experience mod worksheet looks dense, but it's built from a few simple calculations: expected losses from payroll, actual losses split at the split point, and a weighted comparison of the two. Read it top to bottom, check the math, look hardest at claims under the split point, and take anything that doesn't add up to your insurer first.
