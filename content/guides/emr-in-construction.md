# EMR in Construction: Bid Requirements, Laws & Examples

Sooner or later, a prequalification form asks for your EMR. In construction, EMR means your workers' compensation experience modification rate, not an electronic medical record. It's the number your carrier applies to your workers' compensation insurance premium, and many owners use it when screening bidders.

This guide covers what owners actually ask for on real prequalification forms, the states that restrict EMR cutoffs in bidding, what pushes a contractor's mod over 1.0, and how to get ready before bid season. For the full formula, see our guide to [how the experience mod formula works](https://emodanalyzer.com/what-is-experience-modification-rate-emr/).

> **Key Takeaways**
>
> - There is no universal EMR cutoff. On the public forms we reviewed, one California agency requires a full-time safety person above 1.25, while North Carolina's state construction office scores EMR and safety record as one part of a 100-point prequalification form.
> - Texas, Virginia, and Indiana restrict hard EMR cutoffs in bidding, and a national model act adopted in November 2025 encourages other states to follow.
> - Frequency is what usually pushes a contractor over 1.0. On NCCI's sample employer, one huge claim tops out at a 0.90 mod, but fifteen $5,000 claims push it to 1.01.
> - Get your rating worksheet early and check it before the bid package goes out. Errors are easier to fix before an owner sees the number.

## What Does EMR Mean in Construction?

EMR is your experience modification rate: a multiplier on your workers' comp premium that compares your claims with the claims expected for contractors of your size and class codes. An EMR of 1.0 means your claims match the industry average for similar construction businesses. Below 1.0 is a credit that lowers your premium, and above 1.0 is a debit that raises it. You'll also see it called an experience mod, e-mod, X-Mod, experience modification rating, or EMR score. In most states, your EMR is calculated by the National Council on Compensation Insurance (NCCI) from three years of your payroll and claims.

Construction feels the mod more than most industries because construction classification codes carry high rates. In NCCI's own example in its [ABCs of Experience Rating](https://www.ncci.com/Articles/Documents/UW_ABC_Exp_Rating.pdf), the rate for roofing was $63.17 per $100 of payroll, against $0.75 for clerical work. With $126,865 of premium, a 1.25 mod raised that roofing company's bill to $158,581. On that company's $525 of clerical premium, the same 1.25 mod added only about $131.

## Why Do Owners and General Contractors Ask for Your EMR?

Owners ask for your EMR because it's a standardized number, calculated by NCCI or your state's rating bureau, that reflects several years of claims, so it works as a quick safety metric for comparing construction companies. They rarely look at it alone. Public prequalification forms usually pair it with other safety data, such as OSHA injury rates, and ask for supporting documents:

- **Several years, not one.** California's Department of Water Resources (DWR) asks for your current EMR and the three years before it in its [safety prequalification questionnaire](https://water.ca.gov/-/media/DWR-Website/Web-Pages/Programs/Engineering-And-Construction/Files/Construction-Contracts/SPQ_July_2019_0620.pdf), which contractors refile every year.
- **The rating sheet itself.** The California State University system's [prequalification guide](https://www.calstate.edu/csu-system/doing-business-with-the-csu/capital-planning-design-construction/Documents/pq-instruction-guide.pdf) asks for the WCIRB or California intrastate EMR, plus a copy of the current experience rating form from the WCIRB, California's rating bureau.
- **OSHA logs alongside it.** The same CSU guide requires three years of OSHA Form 300 and 300A, and DWR asks for recordable and lost-workday incidence rates.

An EMR isn't a pure measure of safety performance, though. The International Risk Management Institute (IRMI) has pointed out that the modifier is "influenced by external factors such as advisory rates, payroll, changes in the experience modification formula," as quoted by [Business Insurance](https://www.businessinsurance.com/indiana-becomes-latest-state-to-ban-e-mods-in-construction-bidding/). That's one reason some states have stepped in, as covered below.

## What EMR Do You Need to Bid?

There is no single number, and we found no primary source that publishes EMR benchmarks or an average EMR by trade. The public documents we reviewed handle EMR in at least three different ways: as a trigger for extra requirements, as a default for firms without a rating, or as one factor among many.

None of these forms says "1.0 or below or you're out." North Carolina's [prequalification presentation](https://www.doa.nc.gov/documents/files/sco-scc-2017-prequalification-process-presentation-new-atlantic-pdf/open) treats EMR and safety record as one scored item among several. DWR's line is 1.25, and crossing it adds a staffing commitment rather than ending your bid. Private owners and general contractors may set stricter EMR thresholds in their own documents, so read the actual bid package rather than relying on what "most GCs" supposedly require.

**What if you don't have an EMR?** That's common for small or new contractors. NCCI assigns a unity mod (1.0) when a business doesn't meet the premium threshold for experience rating, lacks enough data, or is new with no data. Owners plan for this: CSU tells those firms to enter 1.00, and DWR asks for loss runs instead.

## Can an EMR Cutoff Legally Keep You From Bidding?

In a few states, not for certain bids. Texas and Virginia restrict EMR requirements in some public construction solicitations, Indiana's 2025 law reportedly reaches all projects, and the National Council of Insurance Legislators (NCOIL) adopted a model act in 2025 so other states can do the same.

- **Texas.** Government Code Section 2252.909, added by [House Bill 679](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/88R/billtext/html/HB00679F.htm), took effect September 1, 2023. For construction contracts and public-work contracts awarded by a governmental entity, a solicitation may not require a specified experience modifier to respond, and an offer or contract may not require one either. Terms that break the rule are "voidable as against public policy." It covers solicitations first advertised on or after September 1, 2023.
- **Virginia.** Under [Code Section 2.2-4302.1](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/2.2-4302.1/), no Invitation to Bid for construction services may condition a bidder's eligibility on having a specified experience modification factor.
- **Indiana.** House Bill 1214, signed April 16, 2025 and effective July 1, 2025, says "a party may not prohibit an employer from bidding on a contract solely on the basis of the employer's experience rating" ([Business Insurance](https://www.businessinsurance.com/indiana-becomes-latest-state-to-ban-e-mods-in-construction-bidding/), May 15, 2025). Business Insurance, citing IRMI, reports that it applies to all projects, not only public ones.
- **The model act.** NCOIL's [Experience Rating Modification Model Act](https://ncoil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/NCOIL-Experience-Rating-Model-November-2025-1.pdf), adopted November 15, 2025 and sponsored by Indiana Rep. Matt Lehman, uses the same bidding language.

<!-- [UNIQUE INSIGHT] -->
The NCOIL model, at least, stops hard cutoffs, not scoring: it says it "does not preclude a party from considering an employer's experience rating when awarding a contract." Texas's and Virginia's statutes address what a solicitation may require, so check how each owner evaluates bids. A high mod may still count against you in an evaluation, even where it can't keep you from bidding. If you work in another state, check its procurement rules and the solicitation itself. This section is general information, not legal advice, and reflects the laws as of October 2026.

## What Actually Pushes a Contractor's EMR Over 1.0?

Claim frequency. Each workers' compensation claim is split at a split point: the part below it (primary loss) counts in full, while the part above it (excess loss) is weighted down. A small claim is entirely primary, so a string of small claims moves the mod faster than one large claim.

Every claim below is treated as a lost-time claim, not medical-only, and split points have been set by state since 2024, so your own numbers will differ.) For this employer, no single claim can cross a 1.0 line once the per-claim limit applies, but a steady run of minor injuries can. That's by design: the ABCs guide says the cost of an accident "is often left to chance," while the fact that it happened is more predictable. For a contractor, that means the everyday injuries, not just the catastrophic one, are what usually lead to a higher EMR.

Two more things shape how much your mod moves:

- **Medical-only claims count at 30%.** Most states have approved NCCI's Experience Rating Adjustment, which cuts medical-only claims by 70% in the mod. NCCI says this "decreases the incentive for employers to pay medical-only claims without reporting them to the insurance provider."
- **Company size changes the swing.** The weight on excess losses "is a small percentage for small employers and increases with the size of the employer," and the plan is built to temper "the debit due to such a loss, as well as the credit for having no losses." A small subcontractor's mod stays closer to 1.0 in both directions, while a large general contractor's mod follows its own record more closely.

## EMR vs. TRIR: What's the Difference?

EMR is an insurance pricing factor built from your carrier's claim costs. TRIR (total recordable incident rate) is an OSHA recordkeeping rate you calculate from your own injury log. Owners often ask for both because they measure different parts of your safety performance.

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th></th><th>EMR</th><th>TRIR</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>What it measures</td><td>Your claim costs compared with expected costs for similar employers</td><td>How many recordable injuries and illnesses you had per 100 full-time workers</td></tr>
<tr><td>Who produces it</td><td>NCCI or your state's rating bureau, from carrier data</td><td>You, from your OSHA 300 log</td></tr>
<tr><td>Time period</td><td>About three policy years, excluding the current one</td><td>Usually one calendar year</td></tr>
<tr><td>Formula</td><td>Weighted actual losses compared with expected losses</td><td>Recordable cases × 200,000 ÷ hours worked by all employees</td></tr>
<tr><td>What moves it</td><td>Claim count and cost, payroll, class codes, rating values</td><td>Number of recordable cases and hours worked</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

The 200,000 in the TRIR formula represents "100 employees working 40 hours per week, 50 weeks per year" ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/iif/overview/compute-nonfatal-incidence-rates.htm)), and it's the same formula printed on DWR's questionnaire. DWR compares bidders' rates with the industry average from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). For 2024, BLS reported 2.2 nonfatal injury and illness cases per 100 full-time workers in construction ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/charts/injuries-and-illnesses/number-and-rate-of-nonfatal-work-injuries-and-illnesses-by-industry.htm)). The stakes for construction safety go beyond either number: BLS counted 1,032 fatal injuries among construction and extraction workers in 2024, including 370 from falls, slips, and trips ([BLS Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cfoi.nr0.htm), February 2026).

## How to Prepare Your EMR Before Bid Season

The best time to deal with your EMR is before an owner sees it. Get the worksheet early, check it, and have your documents ready.

<p><img src="images/supervisors-clipboard.webp" alt="Two construction supervisors in hard hats and high-visibility vests reviewing a clipboard on a jobsite" width="1280" height="720" loading="lazy"></p>

1. **Get your current rating worksheet.** Your agent or carrier can provide it, and in some states NCCI also notifies employers directly. In California, the WCIRB issues the rating form that owners like CSU ask for.
2. **Check it for errors.** Look at payroll by class code, open claims still carrying large reserves, medical-only claims (injury type 6) that should be reduced, and any ownership change, which NCCI requires you to report to your insurer in writing within 90 days. Our walkthrough shows how to [check your experience mod worksheet for errors](https://emodanalyzer.com/how-to-read-experience-mod-worksheet/).
3. **Know which claims drive the number.** Because frequency matters most, a few small claims may be costing you more than the one everyone remembers. You can [see which claims are driving your mod](https://emodanalyzer.com/) and what each costs in mod points with the free Experience Mod Analyzer, which reads an NCCI worksheet in your browser.
4. **Have your backup ready.** Keep three years of loss runs and OSHA 300 and 300A summaries on hand, and consider a short written explanation for any year your EMR was above 1.0. Requirements can escalate: DWR, for example, requires a full-time safety person if your EMR or incident rates cross its thresholds, and copies of citations plus an abatement plan if any citation from the last three years hasn't been abated.
5. **Work on frequency.** The ABCs guide notes that experience rating rewards loss prevention and getting injured employees back to work "as soon as reasonably possible." Safety training aimed at your most common injuries, a written safety program, and regular safety inspections are practical places to start if you want to improve your EMR.

Remember the timing: a claim stays in your mod for about three policy years, because mods use policies that started 21 to 57 months before the mod's effective date. Improvements show up gradually.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good EMR rating for construction?

Any EMR below 1.0 is a credit, meaning your claims beat the average for contractors like you. Beyond that, "good enough" depends on the owner. California DWR, for example, adds a full-time safety requirement only above 1.25, while other owners may set lower limits in their bid documents.

### Can my EMR disqualify me from bidding?

It depends on the owner and the state. Owners often ask for your EMR when screening bidders, as the forms above show, but Texas and Virginia restrict EMR requirements in some public construction solicitations, and Indiana's 2025 law bars excluding a bidder solely on its experience rating. The NCOIL model act says explicitly that a party can still consider an employer's experience rating when awarding a contract, and Indiana's law, as reported, uses the same bidding language as the model.

### What if my construction company doesn't have an EMR?

You'll typically be treated as 1.0. NCCI assigns a unity mod when a business doesn't qualify for experience rating or is new with no data, and CSU's prequalification guide tells those firms to enter 1.00. Some owners, like DWR, ask for three years of loss runs instead.

### How long does it take to lower an EMR?

Usually several years. A claim affects about three policy years of mods, so the effect of a bad year fades as that policy drops out of the experience period. Correcting worksheet errors can lower EMR ratings faster.

### Is EMR calculated the same way in every state?

No. NCCI calculates mods in most states, but its plan doesn't apply in California, Delaware, Michigan, New Jersey, or Pennsylvania, which each have a state rating bureau with its own system. New York experience has been rated separately from NCCI's interstate plan since late 2022. Indiana, Massachusetts, and North Carolina produce their own intrastate mods. Four states run monopolistic state funds. Our [guide to experience modification rates](https://emodanalyzer.com/what-is-experience-modification-rate-emr/) explains the state differences.

## The Bottom Line

In construction, your EMR is both a price and a gatekeeper. There's no universal cutoff, some states now restrict hard EMR cutoffs in bidding, and the claims most likely to push you over 1.0 are the small, frequent ones.
